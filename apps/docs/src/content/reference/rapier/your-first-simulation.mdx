In this tutorial we'll build a cannon, aim it at a brick wall, and knock the wall
down. Along the way we'll meet every piece of the package you need for real work:
[`<World>`](/docs/reference/rapier/world),
[`<RigidBody>`](/docs/reference/rapier/rigid-body),
[`<Collider>`](/docs/reference/rapier/collider),
[`<Debug>`](/docs/reference/rapier/debug), impulses, and sensors.

  This guide assumes you're comfortable with the [Your First
  Scene](/docs/learn/getting-started/your-first-scene) basics. For installation and a
  component-by-component overview of `@threlte/rapier`, see the [Getting

## The yard

Before any physics, we need something to knock down. Here's the scene: a cannon, a
brick wall, and a ground plane. Everything is an ordinary mesh, so nothing moves.

<Example path="building-a-cannon/step-1" />

The wall is laid in a running bond, with alternate courses offset so the joints
don't line up. That detail matters more than it looks — we'll come back to it.

## Adding physics

Physics lives inside a [`<World>`](/docs/reference/rapier/world). Anything that
should be simulated goes inside it, and everything else carries on being a plain
mesh.

```svelte title="App.svelte"
<Canvas>
  <World>
    <Scene />
  </World>
</Canvas>
```

Two components make a mesh physical.
[`<RigidBody>`](/docs/reference/rapier/rigid-body) hands it to the simulation, and
[`<Collider>`](/docs/reference/rapier/collider) gives it a shape to collide with:

```svelte title="Scene.svelte"
<T.Group position={brick.position}>
  <RigidBody>
    <Collider
      shape="cuboid"
      args={[0.5, 0.5, 0.5]}
    />
    <T.Mesh castShadow>
      <T.BoxGeometry args={[1, 1, 1]} />
      <T.MeshStandardMaterial color="#9C6B4E" />
    </T.Mesh>
  </RigidBody>
</T.Group>
```

Do that to the bricks and the wall falls straight through the floor:

<Example path="building-a-cannon/step-2" />

This is the single most useful thing to understand about a physics engine, so it's
worth stating plainly: **a mesh is not a collider.** The ground still looks like
solid ground, but we never gave it a body, so as far as the simulation is
concerned there's nothing there. The cannon stays put for the opposite reason — it
has no rigid body at all, so the simulation never touches it.

Note too that the collider's `args` are _half_-extents. A brick drawn with
`<T.BoxGeometry args={[1, 1, 1]} />` needs `args={[0.5, 0.5, 0.5]}` to match.

## Giving the ground a body

The ground never moves, which makes it a `fixed` body: it takes part in
collisions, but gravity and impacts don't budge it.

```svelte title="Scene.svelte"
<RigidBody type="fixed">
  <T.Group position.y={-0.5}>
    <Collider
      shape="cuboid"
      args={[60, 0.5, 60]}
      friction={1}
    />
  </T.Group>
  <T.Mesh
    receiveShadow
    position.y={-0.5}
  >
    <T.BoxGeometry args={[120, 1, 120]} />
    <T.MeshStandardMaterial color="#4E5A46" />
  </T.Mesh>
</RigidBody>
```

Look closely at that group: the collider is offset to `-0.5` to sit where the mesh
sits. A collider doesn't follow its mesh — they're positioned independently, and
lining them up is your job.

Which is exactly why [`<Debug>`](/docs/reference/rapier/debug) exists. Drop it into
the scene and it draws every collider in the world, so you can see what the
simulation is actually working with rather than what you hoped you'd built. Toggle
it on in the example below.

<Example path="building-a-cannon/step-3" />

The wall stands. If a brick topples on its own here, that's not a bug in the
engine — it means the wall as built can't stand, usually because a course is
offset far enough that a brick's centre of mass sits past the edge of the one
below it.

## Firing

Now the fun part. A shot is a ball spawned at the muzzle, given a single shove
with `applyImpulse`:

```ts
body.applyImpulse({ x: Math.cos(aimRad) * power, y: Math.sin(aimRad) * power, z: 0 }, true)
```

Because we push along the barrel's own direction, the aim angle _is_ the launch
angle — set the slider to 30° and the shot leaves at 30°. That's the whole
trajectory model, and it's worth appreciating how little there is to it: gravity
does the rest.

<Example path="building-a-cannon/step-4" />

The ball's `density` is doing quiet but important work here. At this radius,
`density={30}` makes the shot slightly heavier than a brick. Give it a low density
instead and it bounces off the wall like a ping-pong ball — mass ratios decide who
wins a collision.

## Making it behave

The shot works, but it doesn't yet feel right. Four properties fix that:

```svelte title="Scene.svelte"
<RigidBody
  ccd
  linearDamping={0.05}
  angularDamping={0.8}
>
  <Collider
    shape="ball"
    args={[BALL_RADIUS]}
    density={30}
    restitution={0}
    friction={0.8}
  />
```

`ccd` is [continuous collision
detection](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/rigid_bodies#continuous-collision-detection).
Without it, a fast, small body can pass clean through a wall between two
simulation steps — it's on one side at the end of one step and the far side at the
end of the next, having never touched anything. `restitution` is bounciness, `0`
meaning none; `friction` lets the shot bite instead of skating; and the damping
values bleed off the energy that a clipped corner would otherwise turn into a wild
ricochet.

<Example path="building-a-cannon/step-5" />

Try setting `restitution` to `2` and firing again. Physics parameters are much
easier to understand once you've played with the extremes.

## Keeping score

Finally, something to aim _for_. A collider marked `sensor` doesn't push anything
around — it reports what passes through it:

```svelte title="Scene.svelte"
<RigidBody type="fixed">
  <Collider
    shape="cuboid"
    args={[0.15, 2.5, 4]}
    sensor
    {onsensorenter}
  />
</RigidBody>
```

```ts
const scored = new Set<number>()

const onsensorenter = (event) => {
  const body = event.targetRigidBody
  // The shot crosses the line too, so only tagged bricks count.
  if (!body || !body.userData?.brick) return
  if (scored.has(body.handle)) return
  scored.add(body.handle)
  onscore()
}
```

Two guards there, and both exist because of something that actually went wrong.

The `userData` check is the first. A sensor reports **everything** that crosses
it, and the cannonball usually sails straight through — so without this, a shot
that missed the wall entirely still scored a point. Tagging the bricks with
`userData={{ brick: true }}` on their `<RigidBody>` is what tells them apart.

The `Set` is the second. A tumbling brick crosses the plane several times, and
without it a single brick can score four or five times over.

Sensors tell you about every crossing. Deciding which ones _count_ is up to you,
and it's usually more decisions than you expect.

<Tip type="tip">
  Think of the sensor as an invisible pane of glass standing in the gate. The score is how many
  **different bricks have touched that pane** since the last reset. A brick counts if it crosses the
  line or comes to rest against it; it does **not** count if it goes around the outside of the
  posts, sails over the top of the pane, or stops short. Each brick counts once no matter how many
  times it tumbles back and forth, a brick that crosses and then rolls back still counts, and the
  cannonball never counts at all.
</Tip>

Sensors are invisible, so the finish line drawn in the same place is there purely
to show the reader where the pane is — the posts mark its exact width.

## Where to go next

You now have the pieces that most physics work is built from: a world, fixed and
dynamic bodies, colliders, impulses, material properties and sensors. From here:

- **Let Threlte build your colliders.** [`<AutoColliders>`](/docs/reference/rapier/auto-colliders)
  generates collider shapes from your meshes instead of you declaring them by hand.
- **Connect bodies together.** [Joints](/docs/reference/rapier/joints) give you hinges,
  ropes, sliders and motors — a catapult arm, a suspension, a swinging bridge.
- **Decide what can hit what.** [`<CollisionGroups>`](/docs/reference/rapier/collision-groups)
  controls which bodies are allowed to collide — how you'd stop a shot from knocking
  over your own cannon.
- **Turn it into a game.** The sensor and the `Set` in step 6 are already the skeleton
  of a scoring system. Add a limited number of shots, a target to beat and a reset, and
  you have a game loop. [`usePhysicsTask`](/docs/reference/rapier/use-physics-task) runs
  your logic in step with the physics world rather than the render frame, and
  [framerate](/docs/reference/rapier/framerate) covers the fixed-step setup you'll want
  once the rules depend on the simulation being repeatable.
- **Go deeper on the engine.** The [Rapier user guide](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/getting_started_js)
  covers the physics underneath in far more depth than we can here.
